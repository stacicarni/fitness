# Pelvic Floor & Core

The load-bearing concept behind the whole program.

## The canister

Your trunk is a pressure canister: **diaphragm** on top, **pelvic floor** on the
bottom, **deep abdominals (transverse abdominis)** wrapping the front and sides,
**multifidus** at the back. They coordinate automatically — until pregnancy stretches
the front, birth stretches the bottom, and the timing falls apart.

Every exercise in Phase 0 and 1 is teaching the canister to coordinate again. Every
exercise after that is loading a canister that works. That order doesn't reverse.

## Breath is the mechanism

**Inhale:** diaphragm descends, ribs widen 360°, pelvic floor lengthens *down*.
**Exhale:** diaphragm rises, pelvic floor lifts, deep abs draw in.

So: **exhale on the effort.** Push, press, lift, stand — all on the exhale, with the
pelvic floor rising with it. Do this on every rep of every set, forever, and most
pelvic floor problems in lifting never happen.

Holding your breath and bearing down (Valsalva) does the opposite: it drives pressure
straight down into a healing pelvic floor. That's the mechanism behind leaking under
load, and it's avoidable.

## Kegels: the part everyone gets wrong

Two failure modes, and they need opposite treatment:
- **Weak** pelvic floor — can't generate a contraction. Needs strengthening.
- **Overactive/tight** pelvic floor — already gripping, can't release. Needs
  *downtraining*. More kegels make it worse. Common after a long pushing stage.

You can leak from either one. **This is why a PT eval beats guessing.**

Signs you might be dealing with tightness rather than weakness: pain with tampons or
intercourse, difficulty starting urination, constipation, tailbone pain, or a "gripping"
sensation you can't switch off.

**Do the release as deliberately as the lift.** If you can only lift and never
consciously let go, that's the thing to work on.

## Diastasis recti

The linea alba — the connective tissue between the two halves of the rectus abdominis
— thins and widens in pregnancy. **It happens to nearly 100% of pregnancies by the
third trimester.** It's normal, not an injury.

**What actually matters is not the width of the gap.** It's whether you can generate
*tension* across the midline. A narrow gap you can't stiffen is worse than a wider gap
you can. That's why the program never chases "closing the gap" — it trains tension
under progressively more load.

**The rule: no doming.** If a ridge tents up the midline, the exercise exceeds what
your midline can currently manage. Shorten the range, reduce the lever, or regress —
and that boundary will move within weeks.

Recheck monthly with the protocol in Section C5 of the [survey](../intake/INTAKE-SURVEY.md).

**Avoid until well past clearance and no doming:** crunches, sit-ups, V-ups, double
leg lowers, full planks, bicycles, mountain climbers, boat pose, deep backbends,
anything that makes you hold your breath.

**Train instead:** connection breath, heel slides, dead bugs, bird dogs, side planks
(knees → feet), **carries**, Pallof press, and every compound lift done with a good
exhale. Carries and loaded compounds train the deep core better than mat work does —
they just don't feel like "core exercises."

## Reality check on timelines

- **6 weeks:** you're cleared for *activity*, not restored. Clearance is a floor.
- **3 months:** most tissue healing is well along. Coordination still catching up.
- **6 months:** most women feel genuinely like themselves training.
- **12 months:** connective tissue remodeling continues, especially while breastfeeding.

The women who end up strongest at 12 months are almost always the ones who did the
boring version of Phase 0 and 1 properly. That is the entire trick.
